Ten days of H200 kernel optimization for the Muse Spark Open Kernel Challenge

What we built for the five kernel problems, which optimizations paid off and why, the correctness bugs we caught in our own submissions, and what the scores did over time. Oct 22 to Nov 2, 2026.

TL;DR. We wrote custom H200 kernels for all five problems of the Muse Spark Open Kernel Challenge and ended at ×2.47 (routed experts), ×2.52 (attention), ×2.19 (whole decoder layer), ×1.54 (dense projections) and ×1.74 (prefill) over SGLang's own kernels. The biggest single wins were structural: one persistent fused MoE kernel, a split-KV attention kernel whose gather was 2× slower than it should be because of a shared-memory layout bug, and a lossless bit re-layout of the NVFP4 weights that moved dequant work from the saturated integer pipe to the multiply pipe. The late wins all came from reading SASS: ptxas kept recomputing addresses, barrier phases and wgmma descriptors inside hot loops. Along the way we found and withdrew eight classes of latent bugs in our own submitted code (races, out-of-bounds writes, a missing proxy fence), none of which the judge's tests caught. We started near the top of a small board (#16 of 83) and finished around #295 of 1,849: we kept improving, but the field improved faster.

1. The challenge

Meta's Muse Spark Open is a mixture-of-experts model: 256 experts with top-8 routing, NVFP4 (E2M1 codes with E4M3 block scales per 16 values) expert weights, BF16 dense weights, an FP8 KV cache, and grouped-query attention with a 2,048-token sliding window on most layers. The kernel track asks for faster versions of what one GPU does when SGLang serves it on 8×H200 (TP8/EP8):

The judge times each shape as CUDA-graph replays on one H200, with a cold L2 before every replay, fresh inputs each repetition, and paired median ratios against SGLang in the same run; the score is a geometric mean over "loads". The rules matter as much as the hardware: weights keep their precision (lossless repacking only), activations stay BF16 where SGLang keeps them BF16, the FP8 cache stays FP8, attention queries may be FP8 at most, attention must attend every key, and everything must be capturable in a CUDA graph.

2. How the work was done

This was an AI-agent effort: one coordinating Claude session spawned a subagent per problem per round (about a hundred over the ten days), each reading the previous rounds' notes, building candidates, validating them, and using a limited daily budget of H200 checks. The coordinator decided what to submit and set rules for all agents in a shared brief. A few practical details turned out to matter a lot:

  • The local GPU was an H100, the judge an H200. Same architecture, but the H200's extra memory bandwidth (4.8 vs 3.35 TB/s) shifts kernels from memory-bound to issue-bound. Several optimizations that won locally lost on the H200; one agent counted five such flips for the same kernel family. Keeping data L2-resident locally (to take HBM out of the picture) predicted H200 behavior much better than plain local timing.
  • Judge noise is ±1.5-2% between machines, more than most late-stage gains. Decisions were made from in-run A/B checks (old and new kernels timed side by side in one judged run, with an identical-code control), not from whole-file scores.
  • One shared GPU, many agents: every GPU command went through a file lock, with an inner timeout so a deadlocked kernel couldn't hold it.

4. The optimizations that paid off

4.1 Routed experts: one persistent kernel, then make the dequant cheap

SGLang runs the experts as a chain: routing alignment, two Marlin W4A16 GEMMs, SiLU-and-multiply, and a post-expert RMSNorm plus weighted sum. At decode sizes every one of those launches costs latency, and Marlin isn't tuned for a handful of tokens per expert.

  1. One persistent launch per call (×1.43). Every block recomputes the routing itself (cheap), then works through GEMM1 + SwiGLU, GEMM2 and the norm-and-combine. Grids are sized for the worst case so the kernel is graph-capturable even though routing is data-dependent.
  2. Producer/consumer bulk-copy ring (×1.60 → ×1.96). Weights are repacked in prepare() so each pipeline stage is one contiguous 32 KB bulk copy; one producer warp fills a 3-stage shared-memory ring and 16 consumer warps dequantize and run mma.sync; work units come from a global queue, largest experts first. For chunks of 25+ tokens at large batch, a wgmma path with register A operands takes over.
  3. A lossless GF(2) bit re-layout of the weights (+1.8%). Profiling showed the decode inner loop was bound by the integer ALU pipe (~72% issue). On sm_90 that pipe (LOP3, SHF, PRMT) is half rate and separate from the FMA pipe, which also executes IMAD. Shifts can be written as multiplies, but only if the bits sit in the right places. An exhaustive search over invertible bit mixes of the stored 32-bit words found a layout where 8 E2M1 codes decode to BF16 with 4 LOP3 + 1 SHF on the ALU pipe and 3 IMAD.SHL on the FMA pipe, instead of 5 LOP3 + 2 SHF + 2 IMAD. The stored bits are a permutation (decoded weights are bit-identical), applied once in prepare(), independent of the weight values. The same idea came from pipe accounting in some outside advice and then carried straight into the decode-layer and prefill problems.
  4. SASS audits of the hot loops (+1.5%). ptxas rebuilt shared-memory addresses and the wgmma descriptor in every stage; the compiler warp-aggregated our work-queue atomics, and the shuffle after the aggregate stalled the producer for a full round trip (an address offset of tid >> 20, always zero, stops the aggregation); combine paths had 64-byte local-memory stacks. Fixing those cut the wgmma loop from 346 to 299 instructions per stage.

Dead ends (measured on the H200): prmt lookup tables for the dequant, applying block scales on the FP32 accumulator, 128-token hot-expert chunks, speculative weight prefetch before routing, cluster-owned down-projection rows, deeper rings at most sizes. The 9-instruction (later 8) dequant per 8 codes is at an exhaustively searched floor.

4.2 Attention: a split-KV kernel, and a 2× gather bug

SGLang's path is QK-norm, RoPE, cache write, FA3 over the FP8 cache, then a gated RMSNorm. We replaced it with one fused kernel per shape (prologue: QK-norm, RoPE, FP8 query, cache write) plus a small combine, splitting the KV range across CTAs so a single 80K-context request fills the GPU.

  1. Read each gathered K/V row once for all heads and tokens: with GQA, 16 query heads share 2 KV heads, and an 8-token verify block gives 64 query rows per KV head, exactly a wgmma M dimension.
  2. Cap the grid at one wave of CTAs (×1.10 → ×1.36): tail effects dominated.
  3. The 128B-swizzle fix (the largest part of ×1.69 → ×1.97, together with new verify and decode kernels in the same round). Verify on full layers ran at 2.5× its floor. Turning off exp, the V conversion or the MMAs barely helped; a loads-only run took as long as the full kernel. The K tile's shared-memory layout made every 16-byte copy its own transaction and fetched each 32-byte sector twice. Storing K in a 128B-swizzled layout fixed it.
  4. No separate combine kernel on sliding layers (×1.97 → ×2.21): sliding shapes carry most of the weight and were pure latency (a 2-4 µs combine on a sub-µs data floor). They now merge splits through 16-CTA thread-block clusters (DSMEM) inside the kernel.
  5. A length-balanced split for verify on full layers: the kernel ended with whichever SMs drew the longest request, so one warp now computes balanced split points from the sequence lengths in-kernel.
  6. FA3's softmax convention (max offset 8, exact per-tile max computed before the exponentials) on the sliding/verify kernels: closer to stock numerics and 2-9% faster there.
  7. SASS audits (×2.47 → ×2.52): ldmatrix and V-store addresses re-derived from the thread index at every loop back-edge, per-stage address math in cp.async, and a combine that issued its 34 partial loads one at a time behind ~15 instructions of predicate math each.

What's left is hardware. Random 128-byte row gathers (page size 1) top out around 4.0 TB/s on the H200, and decode on full layers sits at that ceiling: a memory-only probe showed that even perfectly balanced CTAs don't finish sooner, because early finishers just hand their bandwidth to the rest. Verify is latency-bound, not exp-bound (the MUFU/XU pipe is ~60% busy). Dead ends: FA3-style ping-pong, an XQA-style softmax/PV warpgroup split (+60% instructions), FA4-style software exp2 on the FMA units, last-arriving-CTA in-grid merges, two-level merges without the PDL combine, TMA gathers, address sorting, L2 prefetch hints.

4.3 Dense projections: lossless 12-bit weights and lean streaming loops

  1. The router was the first big win: SGLang runs an FP32 matmul at up to 8 tokens; the rules allow the FP32 weights as a BF16 hi + lo pair with FP32 accumulation, so one kernel reads both halves in one pass (1.5-3.8× on router shapes).
  2. Lossless 12-bit weights. These BF16 weights use only about 16 exponent values, so each weight is stored as its low byte plus a 4-bit sign/exponent code, with exact out-of-range escapes: 25% fewer bytes at memory-bound decode sizes.
  3. Register-streaming kernels with cluster split-K: weights stream straight into registers in tensor-core fragment order, and the K-split partials are summed across a 2-3 CTA cluster through distributed shared memory instead of global memory. These designs follow the approach published by another competitor in an MIT-licensed repository (github.com/NathenFernandes/muse-bf16-proj); we reimplemented them and credit it in our files.
  4. The load-path fix (+1.7%): ptxas recomputed weight and x addresses from the thread index every unit and kept 8·MT register copies of x; one pointer per tile advanced per unit (kept live with an empty asm "+l") removed 35-45% of instructions per unit.

At m = 128/256, pinned cuBLASLt algorithms won almost every comparison against our own kernels. Those shapes are bound by the weight stream, and split-K partial traffic costs as much as the main loop saves. One lean wgmma kernel finally beat cuBLASLt on the o projection by about 2%.

4.4 Decode layer: composition, glue and launch latency

The whole layer composes the three kernels above with fused glue: QK-norm/RoPE/cache write in the attention prologue, the pooler and router as one GEMM, routing plus pre-expert norm in one kernel, residual boundaries in cluster kernels. Every kernel uses programmatic dependent launch (PDL) and signals its dependents at entry, so the next kernel's prologue (weight loads) overlaps the current kernel; in the measured chains each dependent link still costs ~1 µs.

One judgement call shaped this problem's late scores. With plain FP8 queries, near-tie top-8 router choices flipped in about 1 of 4,000 inputs, enough to fail the judge's routing tolerance roughly once per 30 runs. We switched the layer's attention to pairs of FP8 queries (a high part plus an FP8 residual, about BF16 accuracy, strictly finer than the allowed FP8) and accepted about 1% of score for zero routing failures in 28,800 inputs.

4.5 Prefill

Prefill is compute-bound: the expert GEMM became a wgmma grouped GEMM that decodes NVFP4 in registers (610-640, later ~770 TFLOP/s at 16K tokens), and an FP8 prefill attention kernel replaced FA3 on full layers (4-10% faster and closer to an exact FP32 reference). The GF(2) weight layout, double-tile ring slots and an evict-first L2 policy on the weight stream added the last few percent.

6. Lessons

  • Read the SASS. The highest-yield work in the second week was not new algorithms but finding what ptxas did to hot loops: recomputed addresses and descriptors, divides by a runtime stage count, local-memory spills, warp-aggregated atomics, CTA-wide barriers that wait on unrelated loads. Each fix was bitwise-identical and worth 0.3-2%.
  • Do the pipe accounting. "Issue-bound" hid an integer-pipe bottleneck that the multiply pipe could absorb. The CUDA guide's per-pipe throughput table is the starting point.
  • Measure the bound before building the fix. A memory-only probe, an L2-resident proxy, or a build with a stage stubbed out told us in one check whether an idea could pay (decode-full balancing could not; the gather bug could).
  • Precision decisions are product decisions. We declined a lossless 6-bit block-scale encoding that only worked because the synthetic weights had a narrow scale range, a precomputed derived weight matrix, and adopting other competitors' published code, and we paid ~1% for routing-safe queries. Each cost score; each made the entry more defensible under review on held-out data.

7. Serving

The serving track (Nov 5-19) scores a patch to SGLang on a whole 8×H200 node by throughput at stock interactivity, with outputs required to agree with stock about as well as stock agrees with itself. Our patch brings the kernels above into SGLang behind startup self-checks against stock modules, with fallbacks agreed across all ranks, stock-rounding emulation in the MoE and FP32 split partials in attention so numerics stay close to stock.
