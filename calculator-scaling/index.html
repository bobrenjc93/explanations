Scaling laws for a calculator: parameters, data, and where it stops

Follow-up to the 14-level benchmark and the generalisation follow-up. The question this time: if the model and the data are scaled up, does performance follow, is there an asymptote, or can it just keep going? One task, one architecture family, three knobs turned one at a time: parameters, training length, and the number of distinct problems in the training pool.

TL;DR.

  • Parameters. At a fixed 10,000 steps, in-range error falls from 29.8% at 0.8M parameters to 1.3% at 19.0M. Above the floor it follows a power law in parameters with exponent 1.08: ten times the parameters cuts the excess error by a factor of 11.9.
  • Compute. Size and length are close to interchangeable: over all 6 runs, excess error is training compute to the power -1.11, and no run misses that line by more than 5%.
  • The asymptote in range is the scratchpad's, not the model's. A perfect solver following the worked solutions scores 99.7%. The curves bend toward that floor and nothing else stops them.
  • One size up does have an asymptote, and it is low. Accuracy on problems one size beyond the training range rises from 3.9% at 0.8M parameters to 34.0% at 10.7M and is no higher at 19.0M (32.9%), while in-range error keeps falling. Scale buys accuracy inside the range it is trained on; it does not buy the next range.

Runs still training as this version goes up: 7 of 13. The figures show what has finished; the text and the fits will be updated as the rest come in.

1. What is scaled, and what is measured

Everything is the 14-level benchmark: 14 levels from two-digit addition to Newton's method on cubics, a thinking block with a full worked solution before every answer, 2,000 held-out problems per level. The training pool has 28M problems, 2M per level. The model is the same pre-norm transformer with rotary positions and a looped middle block at every size; only width and depth change.

  • Parameters: six sizes from 0.2M to 19.0M (width 64 to 512, 4 layers up to 3.2M and 6 above), each trained for 10,000 steps of batch 2,048.
  • Training length: the 3.2M model for 2,500, 5,000, 10,000 and 30,000 steps, and the 0.8M model for 10,000 and 30,000. Each run has its own warm-up and decay, so these are separate runs and not points read off one curve.
  • Distinct data: the 3.2M model for 10,000 steps on a random 0.2%, 2%, 20% and 100% of the pool. The number of problems drawn is the same 20.5M in every run; what changes is how many different ones there are to draw.

Three things are measured at the end of every run. In-range error: the share of the 28,000 test problems answered wrong, generated greedily. Held-out loss: cross-entropy per token on the test problems' reference solutions. And one size up: exact match on 100 problems per tier from the stretch set of the 14-level benchmark, where every tier's size is one step past its training range. One seed per run; in earlier write-ups a second seed moved level-averaged accuracy by up to 2 points, so small differences between neighbouring points mean little and the slopes mean more.

2. Parameters

Error over the 14 levels goes from 29.8% at 0.8M parameters to 1.3% at 19.0M. Measured above the floor, the fall is close to a straight line on log-log axes: a power law with exponent 1.08, so each tenfold increase in parameters divides the excess error by about 11.9. The new tiers start further behind (37.8% error against 21.9% on the old levels at 0.8M) and close the gap as the model grows (1.3% against 1.3% at 19.0M).

By level the picture is a set of thresholds more than a smooth slide. The 0.8M model has something on every level; each tier switches on at its own size and then saturates. The last to come in is L7 composite (95% at 19.0M). The smooth power law is what these thresholds add up to.

Exact match on a long solution is a harsh measure, because one wrong token anywhere is fatal. Turning it around gives the rate the model is actually being asked for. If a solution of T tokens is right with probability (1 - p)T, then p is the rate of fatal slips per token:

From 1,627 fatal slips per million tokens at 0.8M parameters to 45 at 19.0M. A 1,700-token Newton solution is right 99% of the time only if fewer than 6 tokens in a million go wrong, and that is the regime the larger models are in. This is why the longest tier is not the hardest: its tokens are easy ones, and the slip rate on easy tokens falls fastest.

Held-out loss tells the same story on the axis scaling laws are usually stated on: 0.014 nats per token at 0.8M down to 0.0021 at 19.0M, an exponent of 0.57 over the sweep, flattening at the top where the unpredictable root estimates start to dominate what is left.

3. Training length and compute

Put every run on one axis, the compute it used, and they line up. Across 6 runs that differ in size by a factor of 24 and in length by a factor of 3, excess error is compute to the power -1.11, and no run is off that line by more than 5%. To a first approximation the error depends on how much compute was spent and not on whether it went into parameters or into steps.

The cleanest pair is at 687 PFLOP: the 3.2M model for 30,000 steps (2.3% error) and the 10.7M model for 10,000 (2.1%). The trade is not free at inference: the larger model costs more per problem every time it is used, the longer training is paid once.

4. Is there an asymptote?

In range there is one, and it is known in advance. The worked solutions carry six significant digits, and a solver that executed them without a single slip would still get 0.3% of the test problems wrong in the fourth digit. That is the dashed line in the figures, and it belongs to the scratchpad, not to the model. Above it, nothing in the curves looks like a second plateau.

The check is to fit on the smaller models and predict the largest. A power law through the excess error of the 4 sizes up to 10.7M (exponent 1.09) predicts 1.2% error for the 19.0M model. It measured 1.3%. The law holds one step further out than it was fitted on.

Read forwards, the fit over all sizes says 1% error in range would take about 25.6M parameters at this training length, and 0.5% about 79.9M. Those are extrapolations of a line through 5 points, with one seed per point, and the last points sit close enough to the floor that the exponent is sensitive to where the floor is put. The qualitative answer is firmer than the numbers: in range, more parameters and more steps keep paying until the scratchpad's own precision is what is left.

5. Does scale buy the next size up?

The generalisation follow-up found that these models interpolate over problem size and fall off a few sizes past the largest one they trained on. The scaling question is whether that margin grows with the model.

Up to a point. One size up reads 3.9% at 0.8M, 9.1% at 1.8M, 13.4% at 3.2M, 34.0% at 10.7M, 32.9% at 19.0M. The 19.0M model cuts in-range error by a further factor of 1.6 over the 10.7M one and is no better out of range. In-range error fell by a factor of 23 over the sweep; out of range, error went from 96.1% to 67.1%.

The tiers explain the shape. L9 long expressions and L11 quadratics respond to scale: there a larger problem is the same procedure on larger numbers or a longer chain of familiar steps, and a model whose arithmetic is more reliable gets further. L8 big numbers, L10 linear equations, L12 linear systems, L13 sums stay under 25% at every size: there the stretch asks for more digits than any carry it has done, more terms to collect, more terms to sum, or a combination never seen, and no amount of in-range accuracy supplies that. With 100 problems per tier a single tier's number can move by several points from sampling alone, so the mean is the thing to read. Two points do not make an asymptote, and this is one seed each; what they do show is that the last factor of in-range error bought nothing out of range. If it is a plateau, it is the out-of-range counterpart of the in-range floor, set by the training pool's coverage instead of the scratchpad's precision. Widening the pool by one size, as in the follow-up, moved it from 30% to 92% with 10,000 steps of fine-tuning.

6. The laws, stated plainly

  • In range, excess error falls as a power of parameters (exponent about 1.1 at 10,000 steps).
  • Parameters and steps are close to interchangeable at equal training compute over the range tested.
  • Distinct data matters until each problem is seen a few times, and not after.
  • The in-range asymptote is the worked solutions' own precision (0.3% error), set by the scratchpad's design.
  • One size beyond the training range improves with scale only up to a plateau around a third, and only in tiers where larger means larger numbers rather than more structure. Range comes from the pool; scale buys accuracy inside it.

Limits of all this: one task, one architecture family, models between 0.2M and 19M parameters, a single seed per point, and width and depth changed together. The exponents describe this calculator. The shape (power laws to a floor that the data format sets, and a much flatter curve out of range) is the part I would expect to carry.
