A calculator benchmark that takes longer to saturate

Follow-up to the calculator at 99%. That benchmark is used up: seven levels, 99.1% exact match. The ask this time was harder and harder problems, equations included, and an eval that takes longer to saturate. Same small transformer, same recipe (a supervised thinking block before the answer), 14 levels instead of 7, and worked solutions of up to 2,300 tokens.

TL;DR.

  • The benchmark. The seven old levels plus seven new tiers: big numbers, long expressions, linear equations, quadratics, linear systems, sums, and Newton's method. 28,000 held-out problems. A reference solution runs from 15 tokens (L1) to 2,300 (L14), and a solver that executes it perfectly scores 98.6% or more on every level.
  • The old eval said nothing about harder problems. The model that scores 99.1% on L1 to L7 gets 11.9% on L8 and 6.2% on L9, which are written in the grammar it already knows and are only bigger.
  • Trained on all 14 levels (3.2M parameters, 10,000 steps), the same architecture reaches 95.4% on the old levels and 91.3% on the new ones, from 99.8% (L13) down to 76.2% (L9).
  • The thinking block matters more as problems get harder. On the new tiers: answer only 13.2%, a block with values only 56.5%, a block with full procedures 91.3%.
  • What does not saturate: one size up. Every tier has a size knob. A stretch set turns each knob past its training range (more digits, more operations, more terms, more iterations). There the 10k-step model scores 15.0%, against 91.3% inside the range. Training on a range does not buy the next one, so this number stays low however long the in-range numbers are polished.

Still training as this version goes up: runs with three times the steps, 3.4 times the parameters, thinking tokens in front of the block. Their sections will be added when they finish; nothing below depends on them.

1. The old benchmark is used up

The last write-up ended with a 10.7M-parameter model at 99.1% exact match over seven levels, from 57+38 to composite TI-84 expressions. A test set where the best model misses one problem in a hundred cannot rank anything that comes after it. The first question is whether that 99% means the model can calculate, or only that it can do the seven things it was trained on.

Two of the new tiers are written in the grammar the old model already reads. L8 is ordinary arithmetic with bigger operands (8 to 14 digits for sums, 4 to 8 for products, long division and Euclid on 7 to 12 digit numbers). L9 is the same kind of expression as L7 with 4 to 9 operations instead of 2 or 3. The old model has never seen a problem this long, and it was not trained on either tier:

11.9% and 6.2%. The procedures are the ones it executes at 99% when the problem is short; it does not carry them further than it was shown. So the old number was a statement about a distribution, and anything harder needs its own test.

2. Seven harder tiers

The new tiers were built to three rules. Each needs strictly more work than the ones before it. The true answer comes from exact arithmetic (rationals, or double precision where a root is involved) and never from the worked solution. And the worked solution, executed perfectly, has to end in the true answer almost always, so the benchmark measures the model and not the scratchpad: the ceiling column is that rate. (In the previous write-up the first scratchpad capped a perfect model at 94.9%, and nobody noticed until the error analysis.)

The display rule is unchanged (integers in full, otherwise 4 significant digits), except that the new tiers show integers in full up to 1018. Equations with several answers list them separated by commas, roots in ascending order; a quadratic with no real root answers none. Each level has 2,000 test problems, 28,000 in all.

What is held out, and two versions that were thrown away. Test problems never appear in training. For most tiers that means the problem text. The first version of the Newton tier showed why that is not always enough: a model trained to give the answer with no thinking at all reached 60% on it after 3,000 steps. The same polynomial appeared in training with other starting points, and the root could simply be remembered. Holding out whole polynomials did not fix it (57%): most of the tier was quadratics with small integer coefficients, and their roots are a table of a few hundred square roots plus a little arithmetic. So the tier is cubics only, held out by polynomial, and quadratics (L11) are held out by root set: no rearrangement, multiple or restart of a test equation is in the training pool. An answer-only baseline turned out to be a good test of a benchmark.

3. A thinking block for an equation

The recipe is the one that worked in the calculator at 99%: before the answer the model writes a thinking block, <think> a worked solution </think>, and in training it is shown what goes in the block. Every arithmetic step in the new tiers is the same kind of step as before: partial products with a running sum, long division digit by digit, a Newton step for a square root, Euclid for a gcd. What is new is the algebra between them, which takes two kinds of line:

  • Restated equations. After multiplying out brackets, after collecting terms, and after moving x to one side, the equation is written again in its new form: 3*x-6+4*x=5*x+5-7, then 7*x-6=5*x-2, then 2*x=4.
  • Labelled values. d=49 for a discriminant or a determinant, dx= and dy= for Cramer's rule, k=3 for the index of a sum, x=1.333 for the current Newton iterate.

The procedures: linear equations are expanded, collected, moved and divided. Quadratics go through the discriminant; the root where -b and the square root add up comes from the formula and the other from x1*x2 = c/a, which avoids the cancellation that cost the textbook formula 3% of its answers at 6 digits. Systems use Cramer's rule, 3x3 determinants by cofactors of the first row. A sum evaluates its term for every k and keeps a running total. Newton's method (cubics, from a given starting point) writes the derivative once, then repeats: f(x) and f'(x) by Horner's rule, their quotient, the new x. The iterate carries 2 significant digits after the first step, 3 after the second and 4 from then on, since each step roughly doubles the correct digits anyway, and the loop stops when x no longer changes at 4 digits. 4. Results inside the training range

Every run trains on a pool of 28M rows, 2M per level, drawn uniformly, at batch 2,048. The model is the 3.2M-parameter transformer of the earlier write-ups (4 layers, the middle two applied twice, rotary positions) unless a row says otherwise. Trained on all 14 levels for 10,000 steps, the full-procedure model holds 95.4% on the old levels (the single-benchmark model had 96.6% at this budget, with all of its training on those seven) and reaches 91.3% on the new ones. The spread is the point: L8 84%, L9 76%, L10 96%, L11 96%, L12 94%, L13 100%, L14 93%. The weakest is L9 long expressions.

The thinking block does more work here than it did before. Without one the model scores 48.6% on the old levels and 13.2% on the new tiers; with values only, 74.5% and 56.5%; with procedures, 95.4% and 91.3%. On the old levels procedures were worth 21 points over values; on the new tiers 35.

Sums (409 tokens on average) and Newton's method (1,700) come in early; scientific functions, composite expressions, big numbers and long expressions come in last. A Newton iteration, by contrast, forgives: the next iterate repairs a small error in the last. (The dip at step 4,000 is the optimiser at its peak learning rate, not the eval: every level falls together and recovers by the next evaluation.)

6. One size up: the stretch set

Section 1 retired the old benchmark with one test: problems of a familiar kind, a size larger than any the model had trained on. The new model gets the same test. Every tier has a size knob, and the stretch set turns each one past its training range: 300 problems per tier, never trained on, with reference solutions of up to 4,000 tokens. Nothing about the procedure changes. A 13-term sum is evaluated exactly like a 12-term one.

The 10k-step model falls from 91.3% inside the range to 15.0% one size up. It holds up best on L11 quadratics (44.3%) and worst on L12 linear systems (0.0%). The old model fell from 99% on its own levels to 12% on L8; this one falls about as far going from the range it trained on to the next.

The knob curves show how close the edge is. A sum of 13 terms, one more than any sum in the pool, is solved 72% of the time; 16 terms, 19%; from 18 terms on, almost never. Long expressions and linear equations step down the same way at their first unseen size.

The figure separates two things that are easy to confuse. Inside the range, accuracy hardly depends on how long the solution is. Beyond it, accuracy is low at almost every length, short solutions included: a stretch problem whose solution takes 300 tokens is much harder than an in-range problem of 300 tokens. What fails is not stamina. It is doing a familiar procedure at an unfamiliar size.

Each tier breaks in its own way. Big numbers go wrong in the very first step: a few more digits than it has seen are enough to break a carry, a partial product or a division. Linear equations lose terms while collecting a dozen of them. The sums are the clearest case of a procedure that was not really learned: three quarters of the failures are solutions that simply stop early, nearly always before the 13th term, without looking at the upper limit. A training sum never had more than 12. And Newton's method fails where the output becomes longer than anything the model ever wrote: 57% of its first faults fall after token 2,000 of the solution, next to the 2,304 where every training solution had ended.

This is the part of the benchmark that takes longest to saturate, because training on it does not use it up. Add the stretch range to the training pool and that range will be learned; the set one size above it is then the new stretch set, built by the same generator with the knobs turned again. A fixed test set measures whether a model has learned a distribution. A stretch set measures whether it has learned the procedure, and for these models the answer is: not yet.

7. Is it the length?

When this started I expected the natural score to be a horizon: the length of worked solution at which accuracy falls to some threshold, read off a curve of accuracy against solution length. Every problem has such a length, independent of any model: the number of tokens in its reference solution. Here is that curve for each run, over all 28,000 test problems.

For the weaker formats the horizon is real. Answering with no block, accuracy crosses 50% at solutions of about 41 tokens; with values only, at about 375. With full procedures the curve does not fall off inside the range at all: it dips where the long arithmetic lives (big numbers, long expressions) and comes back up for sums and Newton's method, whose solutions are the longest in the benchmark. Per token, a Newton iteration is easier than a 14-digit carry. So a single horizon number would describe the mix of tiers more than the model, and the honest summaries are the two in the sections above: accuracy per level inside the range, and accuracy one size up.

Two implementation notes for sequences this long. The teacher-forced pass uses fused attention over prompt and output instead of building the attention matrix explicitly, which is the difference between fitting a 2,300-token bucket in memory and not. And decoding writes keys and values into a preallocated buffer; re-concatenating the cache at every token is quadratic in copies.
