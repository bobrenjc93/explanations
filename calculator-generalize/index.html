Does the calculator generalise? Gaps, a wider pool, and a procedure for sine

Follow-up to the harder benchmark, which ended with a model that scores 97.7% inside the sizes it trained on, 27% one size beyond them, and knows sine as a table of 1,999 values. The question left open there was whether these models generalise at all. Three tests, run as one fine-tune: leave sizes out in the middle of the range, widen the range and test past the new edge, and replace the sine table with a procedure.

TL;DR.

  • Between the sizes it has seen: yes. Sizes left out of training in the middle of the range (a 9-term sum when 8 and 10 were trained) are solved 96.7% of the time, against 96.0% for the sizes on either side.
  • Past the largest size it has seen: the edge moves, it does not go away. After the stretch sizes are added to training they go from 30.0% to 92.2%. The sizes one step beyond the new pool go from 2.3% to 28.3%, about where one size up stood before the pool was widened (30.0%).
  • Sine by procedure works where it was trained and fades beyond, instead of stopping dead. With argument reduction written into the thinking block and trained for |x| up to 100, exact match on sin(x) between 10 and 100 goes from 2.1% to 96.1%, on both sides of zero, which is the ceiling of the procedure. Between 100 and 300 it is 60.1%, and beyond 300 35.6%, where the table had 0.0% and 0.1%.
  • The original range is unharmed. The standard 14-level test goes from 97.7% to 98.2%.
  • So: these models interpolate over size and extrapolate poorly. What looks like a learned procedure is a procedure for the sizes covered, plus a fading margin. Each of the three fixes worked about as far as its training data went.

1. One run, three tests

The starting point is the 3.2M-parameter model from the harder benchmark after 30,000 steps: 97.7% on the 14-level test, 26.6% on the stretch set (every tier one size past its training range). It is fine-tuned for 10,000 more steps on a wide pool that differs from the original in three ways.

  • The stretch sizes are added. Every tier is drawn half from its original range and half from one size up. The 2,100 stretch-set problems themselves are kept out, so that set is still a test.
  • A few sizes are left out on purpose. Long expressions never have 7 or 12 operations, linear equations never 6 or 10 terms, sums never 9 or 17 terms. Those sizes sit inside the range, with trained sizes on both sides.
  • Sine and cosine get a procedure. In the original pool sin(x) beyond the 0.01 grid on [-9.99, 9.99] was a recalled value. Now the worked solution reduces the argument first, and half of the direct sin and cos problems have |x| between 10 and 100, both signs.

The reduction is how a calculator does it. Divide x by 2π to one decimal and round to the nearest whole number of turns k; subtract k times 2π, with 2π split as 6.28 + 0.00318531 so the difference keeps its digits; then take the sine of what is left, which is now inside the table's range:

The pool has 15M rows, a mean solution of 401 tokens and solutions of up to 4,096. Fine-tuning used half the original learning rate. The machine restarted under the run three times; it was resumed from its step-7,000 checkpoint, and the last 3,000 steps drew from a fresh 3.75M-row sample of the same generator. Three test sets sit outside it: the stretch set (now inside the pool's sizes, problems unseen), a gap set of 300 problems per tier at the left-out sizes, and a two sizes up set of 200 per tier beyond the wide pool's largest size.

The sizes that were added are learned: the stretch set goes from 30.0% to 92.2% on the six tiers, on problems that were never in the pool. The sizes one step past the new pool go from 2.3% to 28.3%: best on L9 long expressions (54.0%), worst on L8 big numbers (9.5%). The curves show where the fall is.

For sums the picture is the same as before, moved over. The original model is at 100% for 12 terms, the most it had trained on, first drops below half at 14 and has nothing left from 21 on. The fine-tuned one is at 96% for 24 terms, first drops below half at 29 and has nothing left from 31 on. There is a margin of a few sizes past the edge in both cases, and then nothing. Long expressions and linear equations show the same shape: a wider pool buys a wider range and the same slope at its end.

4. Sine with a procedure

In the harder benchmark the model's sine was exact on the 1,999 grid points it was trained on and about 1% right outside them. For positive x it landed near the right value where training had left scattered samples; for negative x it had nothing. The table could not be extended, because nothing in a table says the function repeats. A procedure does say it. The probe is the same as before: sin(x) on a regular grid, now out to ±1,000.

Inside the range the procedure was trained on, it works: 96.8% between 10 and 40 and 95.4% between 40 and 100, where the table had 2.8% and 1.4%. The ceiling is not 100%: the first-order correction after the reduction is good to about five digits, and a perfect solver following it gets 97% to four. The negative side, which had nothing, goes from 0.8% to 95.9%: the reduction handles the sign, so there is no symmetry to discover.

Past 100 the model has to do the same steps on a number with one more digit: a quotient with two or three digits before the point, and a k of up to 159. Exact match is 60.1% between 100 and 300 and 35.6% beyond 300 (within 0.1 of the true value: 67% and 43%). That is a long way below the trained range and a long way above the table, which had 0.0% and 0.1% there. A procedure does not stop dead at the edge of its training the way recall does: the long division and the multiplication by k are skills the model has at other sizes, and some of that carries. But it fades with every extra digit, the same way the big-number tier did.

5. What this says

The three tests give one answer. These models generalise between the cases they have trained on and not beyond them, and that holds whether the case is a problem, a size, or the range of an argument:

  • A held-out problem of a trained size: solved (the 98% of the previous write-up).
  • A held-out size between trained sizes: solved (section 2).
  • A size past the largest trained size: a margin of a few sizes, then nothing, wherever the largest size happens to be (section 3).
  • A function recalled from a table: exact on the table, approximate between scattered samples, nothing elsewhere.
  • The same function computed by a procedure: at the procedure's ceiling as far as its inputs were trained, then fading as the inputs outgrow them (section 4).

The worked solutions look like algorithms, and inside the trained range the model executes them like one. But an algorithm for adding terms does not care whether there are 24 or 25, and the model does. What it has is closer to a very good interpolator over worked solutions: dense where the pool is dense, smooth across small holes, and empty past the boundary. That makes the practical recipe simple and a little disappointing. To get a range, put it in the pool. A procedure in the thinking block is what makes a range learnable at all (the table could never have covered sine out to 100), and it is not what makes it open-ended.

What was not tried: positional schemes built for length generalisation, index hints inside the solutions, and randomised positions, which are the usual suspects for exactly this failure in the arithmetic-transformer literature. They change the model; everything here changed only the data.

6. Compute

The fine-tune is 10,000 steps at batch 2,048 on rows averaging 401 target tokens: 1,075k positions per step, 304 PFLOP, 1.4 hours on one H100. The 30,000 steps before it were 687 PFLOP. A wider pool costs in proportion to its solutions: the same step count on the original pool is 229 PFLOP.
