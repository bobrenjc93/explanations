Should a transformer say "um"? A calculator LLM and a thinking-token ablation

A 3-million-parameter transformer is taught a calculator, from 47+85 up to sin(1.2) and the quadratic formula. Then 32 training runs compare ways for it to think before it answers: filler tokens, discrete and continuous thinking tokens, looped layers, scratchpads. Every number below is measured on those runs (one H100, 20,000 steps each, 14,000 held-out test problems).

TL;DR.

  • Thinking tokens trained end to end barely move exact match. No thinking: 54.1% (seeds 52.5%, 53.4%, 56.5%). Eight "um" tokens: 53.9%. Eight discrete thinking tokens: 54.6%. Eight continuous thoughts: 55.6%. The gaps are no bigger than the seed-to-seed spread.
  • Continuous thoughts do make the model a better estimator. Answers within 1% of the truth go from 68.2% to 74.6% (multiplication/division: 73% to 87%), in every seed. Filler and discrete tokens do not do this (67.9%, 68.6%): the gain needs a continuous channel.
  • The same compute works better as depth than as thoughts. Looping the two middle layers twice at every position, with no thinking positions and no new parameters, costs what 8 thoughts cost and gets 58.5% (seeds 58.4%, 58.5%, 58.6%); two extra ordinary layers get 57.3%.
  • More thoughts and more memory changed nothing. 16 thoughts score 55.5%; a single overwritten slot scores 54.4%; a model trained with a variable budget goes 49.2% → 52.5% with its first thought and is flat from there to 16.
  • The training signal is the bottleneck. Teaching the same 3M network to write its intermediate values (a supervised scratchpad) gives 70.0%, at lower average inference cost than eight thoughts, and takes 3-4 operand sums from 56% to 96%. Distilling that scratchpad into continuous thoughts in four short stages lost the whole gain (54.8%).
  • The curriculum did not help. Arithmetic first, TI-84 last: 54.1%. All seven levels mixed from step 0, same budget: 59.2%.
  • The calculator. Scratchpad, looped middle block and no curriculum at 10.7M parameters: 83.3% exact and 94.5% within 1% on held-out problems; 100% on long add/subtract, 85% on multi-operation expressions, 68% on scientific functions, 59% on composite ones.

1. The idea, and the questions it turned into

People say "um" when the next word needs more time. A transformer has no such option: every token gets exactly one trip through the layers, and whatever was not computed on that trip has to be squeezed into the following tokens. The proposal (from a voice conversation, paraphrased) is to give the model the "um": let it take extra steps before it speaks, and ask what those steps should be made of.

The conversation walked through a design space. Each fork became a set of models here:

2. The task: a calculator, taught in order

The model reads an expression one character at a time and must print what a calculator would show. The rule for the display is fixed: integers in full, everything else rounded to 4 significant digits (rational arithmetic is exact, so 2.5*4 prints 10, and 7/3 prints 2.333). Answers are written most-significant digit first, like a display, which is the hard direction: the first character already depends on every carry.

Seven levels, introduced one after another. A new level takes half of every batch while the earlier ones are replayed in the other half; the last eighth of training mixes all seven evenly.

Every problem is an expression tree with one evaluator, and a separate recursive-descent parser re-reads the printed string to check that the printer's parentheses mean what the tree meant (280,000 random problems, zero mismatches; Python's own eval agrees on the 274,064 it can express). Training draws from a pool of 10.5 million generated problems. The test set is 2,000 problems per level whose expression strings hash into a bucket that training never sees, so every reported number is on unseen expressions. Scoring is exact string match; "within 1%" is also reported because a display that reads 0.9319 instead of 0.932 is wrong by exact match and nearly right as a number.

3. One transformer, many ways to think

The base model is deliberately small so that dozens of them could be trained: a causal transformer with 4 layers, width 256, 4 heads, rotary positions and a 44-character vocabulary, 3.18M parameters. Every variant below is that same network; the thinking mechanisms add between 0 and 0.5M parameters (the 1,024-word thinking vocabulary is the largest). All are trained for 20,000 steps at batch 2,048 (41M problems) with the same data order, optimiser and schedule.

A sequence is the prompt, then the thinking, then the answer. Only the answer characters are ever supervised; the thinking positions get their gradient through the answer.

No thinking. The position holding = must produce the first digit.

47+85=answer:132⏎

"um" tokens. K copies of one learned filler embedding. Extra positions, so extra parallel compute, but the filler itself carries nothing forward.

47+85=umumumumanswer:132⏎

Discrete thinking tokens. At each step the model picks one of 1,024 thinking words and its embedding is fed back in (trained with straight-through Gumbel-softmax). 10 bits per step. Soft tokens feed back the probability-weighted average of those embeddings instead of picking one.

47+85=t417t33t901t5answer:132⏎

Continuous thoughts. The final hidden state goes through a small linear head and comes back as the next input vector: 256 unconstrained numbers per step, never forced through a vocabulary. Each thought is a new position, so later thoughts and the answer can attend to all earlier ones.

47+85=z1z2z3z4answer:132⏎

Written scratchpad (the supervised reference). The model is trained to write the intermediate values in evaluation order, then the answer. This uses labels the other variants never see, and many more tokens.

52*17=scratch:[364,520]answer:884⏎

The answer's first character is always read off the last thinking position (or off = when there is none). Thoughts and answer characters both count their positions from the = sign, so the digit geometry the answer sees does not shift with K.

Counting compute. With a KV cache, the cost of inference is the number of times a transformer block is applied to a position. Call that a token-layer evaluation: about 12·d² multiply-adds each, with attention over the cache adding under 2% at these lengths. A problem with p prompt tokens, K thoughts and a answer characters costs (p + K + a) × 4 of them; a thought that only passes through the two middle layers costs 2 instead of 4. The average test problem costs 65 with no thinking, so 8 full thoughts are +49% compute and a looped middle block is +50% at every position.

4. Results

4.1 The curriculum, and how far a 3M model gets

Each level takes off at the step it is introduced and keeps improving while it is replayed. After 20,000 steps the plain model is a solid four-function calculator on short problems and a poor scientific one: 100% on small sums, 80% on long sums, 54% on multiplication and division, 66% on decimals/powers/roots, 39% on scientific functions, 28% on multi-operation expressions and 12% on composite ones. Level-averaged: 54.1%.

How much is noise? Three seeds of this exact model finish at 52.5%, 53.4%, 56.5%; rerunning seed 0 on the same code gave 55.2% instead of 53.4% (GPU nondeterminism alone). A gap under about 2 points between two single runs is not a finding. Where a variant has several seeds the charts show each one as a dot.

Does teaching in order help? No. Mixing all seven levels uniformly from step 0, with the same 20,000 steps, ends at 59.2% (seeds 58.7%, 59.2%, 59.5%), above every curriculum seed (52.5%, 53.4%, 56.5%). The staged schedule spends its early steps on problems the model masters within a few hundred updates and shows it the hard levels late: composite problems only exist for the last 31% of training. The staircase makes a nice picture; as a recipe at a fixed budget it loses to plain mixing. Every comparison below was run under the curriculum, so the variants are comparable with each other, but none of them is the best this network can do (4.9 repeats the main ones without it).

4.2 What should a thought be made of?

Eight thinking steps cost 49% more inference compute. In exact match they buy almost nothing: every variant is inside or just above the plain model's seed range (52.5%, 53.4%, 56.5%).

The second chart is where a real difference shows. Feeding back a continuous vector makes the printed number land within 1% of the truth far more often: 68.2% without thinking, 74.6% with continuous thoughts (74.5%, 76.1%, 73.2% per seed), 74.8% with soft tokens. Filler tokens (67.9%) and discrete tokens (68.6%) stay at the baseline. So the extra steps are doing arithmetic of a kind, an estimate of magnitude and leading digits, and that estimate only survives the trip to the next step if the channel is continuous. What they do not deliver is the last digit.

The dedicated head is not what does it. Feeding the final hidden state straight back with no head at all, as Coconut does, scores 56.2% exact and 76.4% within 1%. And the soft mix, which the conversation worried would be boxed in by the convex hull of 1,024 embeddings, is as good as the free vector. What matters is that the channel is continuous, not which continuous channel.

The same holds at a looser tolerance: within 10% of the truth, 85.2% without thinking and 89.8% with continuous thoughts. By level the within-1% gain is largest on multiplication/division (73% → 87%) and multi-operation expressions (37% → 46%), the problems whose exact digits the model cannot get.

4.3 Which skills move

Reading across the rows, the problem types fall into three groups.

  • One step, needs precision. Reciprocals (59% → 66% with continuous thoughts), exact division (85% → 91%), 6-8 digit sums (95% → 99%), abs of a signed sum (93% → 98%). This is where continuous thoughts pay off in exact match.
  • Several steps chained. Sums of 3-4 operands, expressions with 3-4 operations, multiplication by a 2- or 3-digit number. No latent variant moves these reliably. Sums of 3-4 operands look like a counter-example for discrete tokens, but it is a lottery every model plays: per seed, no thinking gets 50%, 52%, 67%; discrete tokens 86%, 69%, 56%; continuous thoughts 49%, 55%, 56%.
  • The scratchpad column. Writing the intermediate values fixes exactly the chained group: 3-4 operand sums 56% → 96%, 2-digit multipliers 38% → 96%, 3-digit multipliers 3% → 69%, 4-operation expressions 9% → 48%. (Its 100% on sin/cos/tan is partly a lookup table: test problems are held out as whole expressions, but sin(-0.14) also appears inside composite training problems, where the scratchpad spells out its value.)

4.4 Where to spend the extra compute

More thoughts. Doubling to 16 continuous thoughts changes nothing: 55.5% exact and 74.4% within 1%, against 55.6% and 74.6% for 8. Whatever the thoughts do, they are done doing it early.

Whole model or middle layers? Sixteen thoughts that pass only through layers 2-3 cost the same as eight full ones. They score 54.3% exact and 69.4% within 1%: the estimation gain of whole-model thoughts (74.6%) is gone. In a 4-layer model the first and last layer are evidently part of the computation a thought needs (one seed).

Looped transformer. No thinking positions at all; instead layers 2-3 are applied twice at every position, prompt and answer alike. Same parameters as the plain model, 98 token-layer evaluations (the price of 8 thoughts). It scores 58.5% exact (seeds 58.4%, 58.5%, 58.6%) and 72.3% within 1%: the best exact match of anything trained without scratchpad labels. Looping three times instead of two gives 58.9%, no better.

Or just add layers. Six distinct layers instead of four, no thinking: the same inference cost again, but 50% more parameters. 57.3% exact, 72.2% within 1%.

The conversation ended on this fork: "Do you actually want to create new token positions, or do you want to give the model additional computation without advancing its sequence position?" For exact arithmetic on this task the results side with the second option. Extra depth applied where the digits are, at every prompt and answer character, beats extra positions appended after the = sign.

4.5 Working memory, or just more forward passes?

Two ways to ask. First, take the trained 8-thought model and break things at inference:

Everything collapses. Hiding all but the newest thought from the answer drops it from 55.2% to 4.0%; giving it another problem's thoughts, 1.9%. So in the model as trained the thoughts are not filler: they carry problem-specific content, and the answer reads many of them, not only the last.

Second, the question the knock-out cannot answer: does the model need that memory, or does it merely use what it is given? Train with a single slot from the start, where each thought overwrites the previous one, so there are 8 serial steps of compute but nothing accumulates. Result: 54.4% exact, 73.4% within 1%, against 55.6% and 74.6% for the growing scratchpad.

4.6 The training signal

The conversation flagged it: "There's one major challenge, though: training." Everything above trains the thinking from the answer loss alone, and it mostly fails to discover multi-step procedures. Give the same 3.18M-parameter network the intermediate values as text to imitate and it reaches 70.0% exact (83.8% within 1%). On this test set the scratchpad is not even the expensive option: most problems have little or nothing to write, so it averages 87 token-layer evaluations against 97 for eight thoughts. It costs more only where it helps most (composite problems: 146 vs 125).

The loop from 4.4 stacks with the scratchpad: both together reach 73.7% exact and 86.2% within 1% with the same 3.18M parameters.

The obvious next move is to get the scratchpad's benefit without paying for its tokens: train with the scratchpad, then remove it one item at a time while handing the model continuous thoughts to hold what the removed text used to hold (the idea behind Coconut and stepwise internalisation). Here that did not work: the distilled model ends at 54.8%, back at the no-thinking level.

The curve shows where it goes: each removal costs accuracy that the next 2,000 steps do not win back. The honest reading is narrow. With 2,000 steps per stage and two thoughts per removed item, this network did not learn to carry the removed values in its thoughts. Published successes use much longer schedules; this run says the transfer is not free, not that it is impossible.

4.7 One model, adjustable thinking time

A model trained with exactly 8 thoughts is welded to that number. Ask it to answer after 4 and it scores 14.3%; after 0, 0.8%; let it run to 16 and it drifts down to 29.1%. It never learned to answer early, and nothing told it when it was done.

Training with a random number of thoughts each step (0, 1, 2, 4, 8 or 16) removes the brittleness: one set of weights answers at any budget it saw and only degrades beyond it (50.5% at 24 thoughts, 29.7% at 32). But look at the shape. 49.2% with no thoughts, 52.5% with one, and then nothing: 52.5%, 52.6%, 52.6%, 52.6%. The model does whatever it is going to do in the first thought and treats the rest as a waiting room. The conversation predicted this ("You'd also need to prevent the model from learning to skip the additional computation"), and the answer loss alone does not prevent it. The flexibility also costs accuracy: 52.6% at 8 thoughts against 55.6% for the model that only ever used 8.

That also settles "some mechanism for deciding when to stop" for this model. Decoding after each budget and stopping once the answer's probability clears 0.99 spends 10.4 thoughts per problem on average for 52.5%, no better than always thinking once. Even an oracle that picks the best budget per problem would reach only 54.6%. There is no extra accuracy at higher budgets for a stopping rule to find.

5. The calculator

The final model uses the three things that helped and none of the things that did not: a written scratchpad, the middle block looped twice at every position, and all seven levels mixed from the first step instead of the curriculum. It is scaled up to 6 layers and width 384 (10.7M parameters) and trained for the same 20,000 steps. It prints exactly the right display on 83.3% of held-out problems and lands within 1% on 94.5%. 6. What this says about the idea

  • The "um" itself does nothing. Extra positions holding a fixed filler bought no accuracy at this size. Whatever a pause does for a person, here a thinking step has to carry content to matter.
  • Continuous beats discrete as a channel, for estimating. A vector fed back unquantised lets the model refine sign, magnitude and leading digit over a few steps; ten bits per step and filler do not. None of them produced exact multi-step arithmetic.
  • New positions or more compute per position? More compute per position. A looped middle block (58.5%) beat every thinking-token variant (55.6% at best with several seeds) at the same inference cost and the same parameter count. Thoughts add compute only after the = sign; the loop adds it under every digit of the prompt and the answer, which is where arithmetic happens.
  • Growing scratchpad or fixed state? It did not matter. One overwritten slot (54.4%), a growing scratchpad of 8 (55.6%) and of 16 (55.5%) are the same within noise, and the variable-budget model stopped using thoughts after the first. The trained model does read its whole scratchpad (hide it and accuracy collapses), but it does not need one.
  • Supervision of the intermediate steps is what limits it. The same weights go from 54.1% to 70.0% when shown what to write down. End-to-end gradient through eight latent steps did not find those procedures, and a short distillation did not transfer them.
  • And the easy-to-hard curriculum cost accuracy at a fixed budget (54.1% with it, 59.2% without). Harder skills need the steps more than they need the ordering.

Caveats. One small model family, 20,000 steps, one task. Many variants have a single seed and the seed spread is about ±2 points, so trust the large or replicated effects: the within-1% gain of continuous thoughts (every continuous seed beats every baseline seed), the looped-transformer gain and the no-curriculum gain (likewise, three seeds each), the scratchpad gain, the knock-out collapses, the fixed-K brittleness and the flat variable-K curve. Discrete tokens were trained with straight-through Gumbel-softmax, one of several options. Thoughts were only allowed before the first answer character, never between digits. The distillation stages were short. A 4-layer model has little "middle", so the middle-layer-thoughts result may not carry to deep models.

What I would run next. The loop and the thoughts together (continuous thoughts through a looped block); longer distillation stages with more thoughts per removed item; an auxiliary loss that asks each thought to predict a scratchpad value, so latent steps get supervision without spending tokens at inference; thoughts interleaved between answer digits; and a penalty or curriculum that stops a variable-budget model from front-loading everything into its first thought.
