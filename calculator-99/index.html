A calculator LLM at 99%: the scratchpad mattered more than the weights

Follow-up to the thinking-token ablation, which left the calculator at 83.3% exact match. The ask was to scale it out to 95%+, with more weights and whatever architecture changes help. Same task, same 14,000 held-out test problems, same display rule (integers in full, otherwise 4 significant digits). It ends at 99.1%, and most of the distance was not covered by weights.

TL;DR.

  • 99.1% exact match (99.5% within 1%) with 10.7M parameters, up from 83.3% at the same size.
  • The scratchpad did it, not the scale. A 3.2M-parameter model goes from 77.7% to 98.1% when only what it is trained to write down changes.
  • The old scratchpad capped a perfect model at 94.9%: it wrote intermediate values to 4 significant digits, the precision of the answer. The new one carries 6 digits and spells out procedures: partial products, long division, Euclid, a Newton step.
  • The whole path, one idea at a time (3.2M parameters, 10,000 steps): answer only 54.3%; looped layers 57.1%; a thinking block with intermediate values 73.9%; 6-digit values 75.6%; procedures in the block 93.9%; four targeted fixes 96.6%; then steps and weights to 99.1%. Loss and held-out accuracy curves for every idea are in section 3.
  • Thinking only helps when its content is supervised. Thinking tokens (filler or continuous) and a thinking block of free tokens, trained from the answer alone, leave the answer-only model within 2.3 points of where it was; thinking tokens in front of a full block add +0.2.
  • A thinking-effort dial works but barely pays. One model answers with no, brief or full thinking: 50.5%, 69.9%, 91.9%. Thinking fully only when unsure of the quick answer saves 4% of the inference compute at the same accuracy, and the dial model is 4.7 points behind one trained only on full blocks: a worked solution is already short when the problem is easy.
  • Three bugs in the training signal, each found by reading wrong solutions: a Newton step the model learned to skip, table entries that existed only in the test set, and a division step that asked for a multiply and a subtract at once.
  • Architecture knobs. Rotary positions matter for long worked solutions: no positional encoding costs 6.6 points and learned absolute positions 4.9. RMSNorm and LayerNorm are indistinguishable (94.4% vs 94.3%). The looped middle block is worth 2.3 points.
  • Quantisation. Rounded after training, the 10.7M model keeps 98.9% at 4 bits per weight and falls apart at 3 (78.8%). Trained with ternary weights from the start, a 3.2M model reaches 81.2% (full precision, same budget: 94.3%), and a ternary 10.7M model 94.3%: more weights at fewer bits is a fair trade here.
  • Compute. A worked solution averages 57 characters against 10 for the old scratchpad, 3.1 times the inference compute at equal size. But at equal inference compute (about 0.6 GFLOP per problem) the 3.2M model with worked solutions scores 98.1% and the 10.7M model with the old scratchpad 83.3%: length was a better use of FLOPs than width.

Two measurements on the 83.3% model (10.7M parameters, written scratchpad, looped middle block) set the direction.

In the vocabulary of reasoning models this is a thinking block. The model's full output for the first row is <think>8-4818=-4810;-4810-96=-4906</think>-4906: two delimiter tokens (stored in the code as [ and ]), free text between them, then the answer, and only the answer is graded. The old scratchpad was a thinking block too, with less in it. Section 6 comes back to what else a thinking block can be.

Checks. All 23,860 arithmetic steps the generator writes for the test set were re-verified by an independent checker: zero errors. Because every value is derived only from what is printed, the final answer is the last value rounded to 4 digits, and that agrees with the true answer on 99.78% of test problems (98.65% on composite ones; the rest is 6-digit round-off). One detail mattered: an inexact 6-digit value is never allowed to end in 0 or 5 (it takes the neighbour on the true value's side), so rounding it again to 4 digits cannot be fooled by a false ...50. That moved scientific-function agreement from 99.1% to 100%.

Cost. The model's worked solutions average 57 characters, answer included (the longest reference is 552), against 10 for the old scratchpad. With a KV cache that is 547 token-layer evaluations per problem, against 175 for the 83.3% model of the same size. Training batches are drawn per length bucket, so shapes stay static and padding stays small.

3. The timeline: every idea, layered

To see how things change as the ideas pile up, the whole path was replayed with one model, from answering directly to the final recipe: 3.2M parameters, 10,000 steps at batch 2,048, a pool of 14M problems with all levels mixed. (The very first idea, an easy-to-hard curriculum, was already settled in the first write-up: at 20,000 steps it scored 54.1% against 59.2% without it, so nothing here uses it.) Each idea is added to the kept ideas before it. Ideas that did not help are shown and then left out. Every run logs its training loss every 250 steps and, every 1,000 steps, generates full outputs for 1,400 held-out problems and scores the final answers.

  1. Answer only. No thinking of any kind: the first answer character is produced right after the = sign.
  2. 8 "um" thinking tokens. Thinking tokens with no content: eight copies of one learned filler embedding before the answer. Not kept.
  3. 8 continuous thinking tokens. Thinking tokens with latent content: the model's last hidden state is fed back as its next input, eight times, before the answer. Not kept.
  4. Unsupervised thinking block. A thinking block of 8 free tokens from a 1,024-word thinking vocabulary. The model chooses them; only the answer is graded (straight-through Gumbel-softmax). Not kept.
  5. Looped middle block. Layers 2-3 applied twice at every position. No new parameters, no thinking positions.
  6. Supervised thinking block: values. <think> the intermediate values, to 4 significant digits </think> then the answer. This is the first write-up's scratchpad.
  7. 6-digit values, one step per operation. Inside the block every operation is restated with its result (93*88=8184) and values carry 6 significant digits. No procedure is written out.
  8. Procedures in the block. Partial products with running sums, long division, Euclid for gcd/lcm/mod, a Newton step for square roots, repeated multiplication for powers, mantissa tables for ln/log.
  9. Shared lookups, off-grid corrections. 10x and 2x from a 100-entry table for the fractional part; sin, cos and ln away from their tables by a first-order correction.
  10. Jittered Newton, e^x split, nCr chains. Root estimates perturbed in training; ex split into whole and fractional part; n!, nPr, nCr as running products; cube roots by Newton.
  11. Products written in long division. 9p7767r101 instead of 9r101 when the divisor has 3 or more digits.
  12. Pool weighted to hard levels. Training rows per level in the ratio 1:2:3:3:3:5:5 instead of equal.
  13. 8 "um" tokens before the block. The filler thinking tokens again, now in front of a full thinking block. Not kept.
  14. 8 continuous thoughts before the block. The latent thinking tokens again, in front of a full thinking block. Not kept.
  15. Thinking-effort dial (at full effort). One model trained to answer with no block, a brief block (values only) or a full block, selected by the token that opens the block. Shown here with the block forced to full. Not kept.

What the timeline says.

  • Thinking that carries no supervised content does little. On the answer-only model (54.3%), eight filler tokens change exact match by +0.9 points, eight continuous thoughts by -0.3, and a thinking block of eight free tokens the model chooses itself by -2.3. With only the answer graded, none of them finds a procedure.
  • Depth at every position helps a little. Looping the middle layers: +2.8.
  • A supervised thinking block is where it moves. Writing the intermediate values before the answer: +16.8 points. More digits and one restated step per operation: +1.7. Writing the procedures in the block: +18.3, the largest single step on the page.
  • Then four targeted fixes add +2.7 together, a point or less each.
  • Thinking tokens again, on top of the full block: +0.2 (continuous) and -0.1 (filler). Still nothing.
  • The effort dial costs 4.7 points at full effort for the ability to answer with less thinking, an ability that saves only a few percent of the inference compute (section 6).
  • Scale comes last: doubling the steps and then tripling the parameters add the final 2.5 points.

The loss panels show why loss is the wrong yardstick across formats. Per token it falls sixfold when procedures arrive, because a worked solution is mostly characters that follow mechanically from the ones before. It then rises at the jitter step, where accuracy improves: a jittered root estimate is random by construction, so part of every such target can never be predicted, and that floor is what the curve sits on. It rises again per problem with the weighted pool, which simply contains longer problems. Where the format does not change (the thinking-token panels, the loop) the two curves are directly comparable, and they lie on top of each other. Every version is still descending at 10,000 steps, which is why the 20,000-step run gains 1.5 points.

5. Three things that broke, found by reading wrong solutions

The first version of the worked solutions already put the 3.2M model above 95%. A tool that generates every test solution and finds the first step that differs from the reference showed three separate ways the rest went wrong. None was a capacity problem.

A Newton step the model never took

Square roots were written as a 4-digit estimate, one long division, and the average of the two. In the training data the estimate was always the correctly rounded root, so the quotient was always a hair away from it, and the model stopped dividing: it wrote plausible quotient digits near whatever it had guessed. When its estimate matched the reference, the answer was right 119 times out of 123; when the estimate was off by even one unit in the last place, 3 out of 97. The step that should repair a rough guess was being copied through. Fix: jitter the estimate by up to ±5 units of its last digit in training, so the division has to be real and the average has to do its job. Afterwards the model's estimate almost never equals the reference one, and it does not need to: 189 right out of 202 when it differs.

Table entries that existed only in the test set

Test problems are held out by their whole expression. For most functions that is harmless: sin(1.2) the problem is held out, but sin(1.2) the sub-expression still turns up inside composite training problems. For a function whose every use is its own top-level problem, a held-out entry is never seen at all: ex at negative x, 10x, 2x, nCr, cube roots. The first version scored 0% on nCr and 26% on the exponentials while getting 100% on sin/cos/tan. Fix: split each lookup into pieces that are shared across problems. 105.33 is 100.33 (a 100-entry table) shifted; e8.4 is e8·e0.4; nCr is a running product and one division. A held-out problem is then made of parts that were all seen.

Multiply and subtract in one breath

A long-division step was digit r remainder: choose the digit, multiply it by the divisor, subtract, with the product never written. How often a division was the first step to go wrong depended almost entirely on the divisor's length: 0.0% for 1 digit, 1.5% for 2, 17.5% for 3, 8.4% for 4 and 63.5% for 6 (3.2M model, 10,000 steps). In 86% of those the digit was right and the remainder was not. Fix: for divisors of 3 or more digits, write the product: 9p7767r101. The remainder becomes a plain subtraction. Afterwards, at the same size and budget: 3.7% for 3 digits and 28.4% for 6. (Four-digit divisors are nearly all Newton steps, which the model was not really computing before, so there is no fair "before" for them.) In the final 10.7M model a division is the first wrong step 21 times in 3,350.

6. Thinking tokens and thinking blocks

Three different things get called thinking, and the timeline has all three.

Thinking tokens are positions before the answer with no supervised content: a learned filler ("um") or a continuous vector fed back from the model's own last hidden state. The first write-up was about these. Before any block exists they move the answer-only model from 54.3% to 55.2% (filler) and 53.9% (continuous), with within-1% going from 66.9% to 69.6% for the continuous ones: the same small estimation effect as before. In front of a full thinking block they do nothing: 96.8% and 96.5% against 96.6%. One seed each; a seed was worth about 2 points in the first write-up.

A thinking block is what reasoning models do: ordinary tokens between <think> and </think>, then the answer, with only the answer graded at test time. The worked solution is exactly that. What can differ is how the content of the block is learned. Unsupervised: eight free tokens the model picks from a 1,024-word thinking vocabulary, trained only through the answer: 51.9%, against 54.3% with no block. The model does not discover what to write. Supervised: shown what to write, the same model reaches 73.9% with intermediate values in the block and 93.9% with procedures. Large reasoning models get their blocks from pretraining on human text and from reinforcement on final answers; a 3M-parameter model starting from nothing has neither, and the supervision has to stand in for both.

Reasoning models also let you choose how much to think. The same thing here: one model trained on a mixture where each problem appears with an empty block (<think></think> then the answer), a brief block (one value per operation) or a full block (procedures), marked by which of three tokens opens the block. The dial works: 50.5% at 0.18 GFLOP per problem with no thinking, 69.9% at 0.41 with a brief block, 91.9% at 0.66 with a full one. It is not free to train: at full effort it is 4.7 points under the model that only ever wrote full blocks (96.6%), because each effort saw a third of the training rows. Left to choose, it opens a full block 100% of the time.

What it barely does is save compute. The obvious policy is to answer with no thinking and think harder only when the model is unsure of what it wrote, judged by the probability it assigns to its own output. Going up one effort at a time (brief, then full) costs more than giving every problem a full block: matching always-full accuracy takes 0.76 GFLOP per problem against 0.66. Skipping the brief attempt and going straight to a full block does better: 91.9% at 0.63 GFLOP, with 39% of problems answered without thinking. That is a saving of 4%.

The saving is small because a worked solution is already adaptive. A full-effort output averages 16 tokens at level 1 and 146 at level 7, so the problems that can skip their thinking are the ones whose thinking was short anyway: at that threshold 95% of level-1 problems are answered directly and 7% of level-7 problems, and every failed quick attempt on a hard problem is paid on top of its long solution. The brief block is the worst deal: it is 54% as long as a full one and settles only 21% of problems. In a large reasoning model the dial matters because the default thinking is long whatever the question; here the supervised procedure is already about as short as the problem allows.

7. Compute efficiency

The unit. One token passing through one transformer layer costs 12·d2 multiply-adds in its matrix products, 24·d2 FLOPs (attention over the cache adds a few percent at these lengths and is left out). Inference with a KV cache costs that once per token per layer application; training costs about three times that per position (forward and backward) for every position in the batch, padding included. So inference FLOPs per problem = (prompt + solution tokens) × layer applications × 24d2, and training FLOPs = steps × positions per step × layer applications × 72d2.

Inference. Worked solutions make every problem about three times longer, and that is the whole cost: 547 token-layer evaluations per problem against 175 at the same model size. But length is cheaper than width. The 3.2M model writing worked solutions spends 0.65 GFLOP per problem and scores 98.1%; the 10.7M model on the old scratchpad spends 0.62 GFLOP and scores 83.3%. Training. Three things kept the longer sequences affordable. Length buckets: solutions run from 5 to 552 characters, so a batch padded to the longest would process 606 positions per problem; drawing each optimiser step as nine sub-batches of similar length (gradients accumulated, one update) processes 124, a 4.9x saving, with static shapes. Compilation: the forward and backward passes are compiled once per bucket and replayed as CUDA graphs, which removes the per-operation overhead that dominates small models (measured on the final 3.2M setup: 319 ms per step eager, 91 ms compiled). bfloat16 autocast throughout. With the GPU to itself, a 10,000-step run of the 3.2M model takes 15 minutes and the 30,000-step run of the 10.7M model 89. Even so the card is far from saturated: that is 79 and 121 TFLOPS of matrix work on an H100, roughly a tenth of its nominal half-precision peak, because matrices 256 to 384 wide and many small element-wise kernels leave most of it idle. The larger model uses the hardware better, so its FLOPs are cheaper in seconds.

Did each change pay for its tokens? From the ladder in section 3, at fixed steps: going from the old scratchpad to written procedures multiplies training compute by 1.8 (30 to 54 PFLOP) and takes exact match from 73.9% to 93.9%. The later fixes are nearly free in compute: written division products add 5% and the weighted pool 17% (it shifts rows toward the long problems), for +0.8 and +0.4 points.

Positions. With the old, short scratchpad, dropping positional information cost 1.7 points (77.7% vs 76.0%). With worked solutions it costs 6.6, and learned absolute positions cost 4.9. The procedures are all relative lookups, the digit under this one, the remainder two items back, at offsets that slide along a sequence of varying length. That is what rotary positions encode directly, and the losses are concentrated in multiplication and division (level 3: 98% with RoPE, 90% learned, 87% none).

Norm. RMSNorm and LayerNorm give the same result (94.4% vs 94.3%; it was 77.8% vs 77.7% with the old scratchpad). Nothing here depends on mean-centring.

Loop. Applying the two middle layers twice, the trick that won the first write-up's ablation, is still worth 2.3 points with no extra parameters.

9. Weight quantisation

Rounding the transformer-block weights after training, with no retraining: 8 and 6 bits are free at both sizes, and 5 bits costs 0.04 points at 10.7M and 0.4 at 3.2M. At 4 bits the 10.7M model keeps 98.9% and the 3.2M model drops to 93.7%; at 3 bits both break (78.8%, 41.5%); ternary is noise. A worked solution is a few hundred consecutive correct tokens, so a small per-token error rate is fatal, and the larger model has the margin to absorb more rounding. In bits, the 10.7M model at 4 bits (5.3 MB) is smaller than the 3.2M model at 16 (6.4 MB), and more accurate.

Training with the quantiser in the loop does better than rounding afterwards. Four bits costs 1.0 points (93.3% against 94.3% at full precision for the same budget). Ternary weights (−1, 0, +1 and one scale per row), useless when applied after training, reach 81.2%: 13 points under full precision. More weights buy it back: a ternary 10.7M model on the same data and budget scores 94.3%, level with the full-precision 3.2M model, in about a third of the bits (2.1 MB against 6.4 MB).

10. What is left

The final model's remaining errors, by the first step that goes wrong: looked-up trig values (40 of 2,073), powers and their tables (29 of 1,166), long division (21 of 3,350), multi-digit multiplication (11 of 3,144) and addition or subtraction (11 of 11,443). By problem type, what is still missing is mostly what has no procedure yet: tan and atan away from their tables, the fractional part of a negative exponent (the model computes 10-1.16 as 10-2·100.86), and the longest nCr chains. The format's own ceiling is 99.8%.

  • More weights helped, after the format was right. At the final format, 3.2M parameters score 98.1% and 10.7M (with a longer run) 99.1%, mostly through better-memorised tables and steadier long procedures. At the old format the same step went from 77.7% to 83.3%, and a 0.8M model on the new format beats the 10.7M model on the old one.
  • Reading failures beat adding capacity. Each of the three fixes in section 5 came from looking at where solutions first diverged, and each was a property of the data, not of the network.
  • It is slower. The model now writes out arithmetic the way a person does on paper, and pays for every character.

Caveats. One seed for most runs. The architecture ablations ran at 3.2M parameters and a third of the main budget, on the second of three scratchpad versions; the final format differs from it only in the fixes of section 5. Two runs were stopped early when a fix made them obsolete. Scores are exact string match on the calculator display; the within-1% numbers are there for scale.
